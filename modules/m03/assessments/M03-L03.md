# Companion assessment — Service discovery and resilient calls

## Practical exercise

EX-1. A checkout API calls a read-only pricing service through a shared proxy. At peak the proxy receives 1,000 calls per second and may track at most 100 outstanding pricing attempts across the proxy tier. It normally refreshes three cached addresses every 30 seconds. One instance stops just after refresh and the registry becomes unavailable for two minutes. The API has a 500 ms end-to-end deadline and permits three total attempts. The proxy permits two upstream attempts for each API attempt.

Trace the stale-address failure and calculate the maximum upstream attempts. Choose discovery ownership and compare an alternative. Set numeric retry, concurrency, deadline and stale-cache budgets; explain overload and registry-outage fallback and recovery signals. For a stated peak/failure observation window, propose and justify a minimum timely-success rate (or maximum error/shedding budget) for original valid pricing calls. Count each original call once from first submission: only a successful response within its 500 ms deadline counts as timely, while shed, rejected, timed-out, late and unresolved calls consume the budget. Distinguish this from latency measured only for admitted attempts. Use explicit attempt-duration, retry and capacity assumptions to argue whether the 100-slot design can meet the proposed target; if it cannot, explain the trade-off and a credible mitigation. State how the design changes for a charge whose response is lost after commit. Identify a production test that checks both the resource limits and original-call success budget.

## Knowledge checks

### Q1

Why can a registry return an address for a worker that has already terminated?

Answer: Failure detection and removal have not necessarily completed, and consumers may use an older cached list.

Explanation: Probes, lease expiry and client refresh occur on separate schedules.

### Q2

A caller has 300 ms in total. Its first attempt and backoff take 60 ms. What is the maximum remaining time for the next attempt?

Answer: 240 ms.

Explanation: The deadline covers the complete operation, including previous attempts and delay.

### Q3

What extra data can a DNS SRV answer provide beyond a host-address record, if the caller supports it?

Answer: A target host and port with priority and weight.

Explanation: A and AAAA provide addresses for host names, while SRV adds service-location selection information.

### Q4

Compare two total attempts at each of two nested call layers with one layer owning two total attempts.

Answer: Nested layers can make up to four downstream attempts, while one retry owner caps them at two for the same single request.

Explanation: Every outer attempt may trigger the inner attempt budget.

### Q5

When does an intermediary improve discovery ownership, and which new cost must the design address?

Answer: It can centralize selection across heterogeneous clients. It adds a request-path component that needs capacity and failure protection.

Explanation: The intermediary must remain available enough to route calls and requires its own operational attention.

### Q6

A caller times out after issuing a create request. Why is repeating it with a fresh operation key unsafe?

Answer: The server may have committed the first operation, so the second key can create another effect.

Explanation: A timeout describes the caller's observation. Retry with the same key only if a durable deduplication contract covers it. An authoritative committed record confirms the effect; an absent, stale, or pending status alone leaves it unknown. Use a fresh key only after authoritative terminal evidence establishes that the original operation did not and cannot apply.

## Answer key / rubric

One viable answer retains the proxy as discovery owner for consistent selection across clients at the cost of an additional dependency. It excludes the stopped address locally and attempts to refresh. Three API attempts multiplied by two proxy attempts can produce **six upstream attempts**. Give the proxy sole retry ownership with at most two attempts and up to 20 ms of jittered backoff. Pass the remaining deadline from the API to the proxy and cap each upstream attempt at the smaller of 180 ms and the remaining time. Pass that budget to pricing as well.

Count every attempt against the 100 proxy slots. Reject excess calls promptly and suppress retries on explicit overload. A timed-out call can still run at pricing, so use pricing-side admission to bound active work and stop canceled work where safe. Use eligible cached candidates for at most 60 seconds after the last successful refresh, then return a controlled failure during the outage. Monitor proxy outstanding attempts, active pricing work, rejection rate, latency, retries and cache age. Restore traffic gradually after successful refreshes and pricing calls recover.

An in-flight cap protects resources, not the fraction of original calls served. For this illustrative scenario, choose a 95% timely-success target to allow 5% misses during the failure while preserving service for most checkout calls; it is a design choice, not a universal requirement. Define the cohort as the 120,000 original valid calls first submitted at 1,000/s during [0, 120 s), beginning at the last successful refresh and outage start. Each must succeed within 500 ms of its own first submission, including calls whose deadlines fall after 120 s. The target requires 114,000 timely successes; rejects, shedding, timeouts, late and unresolved calls count as misses.

While cached candidates are usable, assume steady saturation and a 160 ms mean proxy slot occupancy per attempt, compatible with the 180 ms remaining-clamped cap. Then 100 / 0.160 is roughly 625 attempt completions/s, an optimistic ceiling on original successes before failures or retries consume attempts, versus 950 timely original successes/s required by the target. This steady-phase estimate is not an exact total for the changing 120-second window. Faster work or more proxy and receiver capacity could address this capacity shortfall, subject to load testing.

Separately, assume no eligible fallback and no retry continuation for originals first submitted after the 60-second cache cutoff: the proxy fails them closed. Even if every one of the first 60,000 originals succeeds on time, at most 60,000 of 120,000, or 50%, can succeed on time. More capacity alone cannot fix that discovery outage; a separately validated alternate discovery path or a justified longer staleness policy with its risks could address it. State when the target is unattainable rather than hiding losses behind a good admitted-only p99. Record one row per original call and join retries to it; report timely success, rejected/shed, late, timed-out and unresolved outcomes against the chosen budget, along with admitted latency and proxy/receiver saturation. At peak, stop an instance and interrupt registry access. Verify both the attempt caps and original-call target, deadline, rejection behavior and cache expiry, then check that recovery does not create a second saturation spike. Accept other explicitly justified targets, assumptions and mitigations.

For a charge, reuse the same operation key only if a durable deduplication contract covers it. An authoritative committed record confirms the charge; an absent, stale, or pending status alone leaves the outcome unknown. Use a fresh key only after authoritative terminal evidence establishes that the original charge did not and cannot apply. An equally explicit client-side discovery design is acceptable if it justifies the ownership choice and accounts for client updates, traffic isolation and discovery failure.

Score 10 points: 1 for stale-address trace and six-attempt calculation; 1 for reasoned discovery ownership and its alternative; 2 for numerical deadline and retry budgets; 2 for overload (1 for a numerical concurrency cap, attempt accounting and bounded admission; 1 for a justified original-call timely-success/error budget and the quantitative consequences of shedding, including a credible mitigation or an explicit infeasibility diagnosis); 1 for maximum staleness and outage fallback; 1 for recovery signals and a production failure test that checks the proposed original-call budget as well as attempt limits; 2 for the ambiguous charge outcome and safe retry contract. A 100-slot cap or good admitted-only latency without original-request outcome accounting earns no error-budget point. Do not require a particular universal success threshold: assess consistency with the chosen window, capacity and failure assumptions. Full credit in each criterion requires a coherent mechanism and consequence or verification; an unqualified plausible choice earns at most half. Accept other justified budgets and either ownership model when they respect the given constraints.
