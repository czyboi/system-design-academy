# Reference request path and rubric

## DNS, connections and routing

The browser asks its recursive resolver for the public hostname. On a miss, the resolver follows referrals and obtains the authoritative answer. In the DNS incident, R1's old answer expires at +90 s, so at +60 s it still supplies edge A. R2 queries and gets edge B. Existing connections, additional application caching, or a configured serve-stale fallback when refresh fails can produce a different observed migration timeline. Confirm which destination address new connections actually use. [RFC 1034 §5.3.3](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc1034#section-5.3.3), [RFC 8767 §4](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc8767#section-4)

The browser connects to the edge using HTTP/3 over QUIC when negotiated and reachable, or HTTP/2 over TLS and TCP for capable clients. The edge terminates browser TLS. It serves an eligible cached response on a hit. On a miss or for private traffic, the edge makes a separate encrypted connection to the origin ingress, for example pooled HTTP/2 when supported. The ingress terminates that TLS session and selects the media or saved-items pool by HTTP path. Backends and network trust boundaries have explicit encryption and authentication policies. The edge returns the response on the browser connection. [RFC 9113 §5](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc9113#section-5), [RFC 9114 §2](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc9114#section-2)

L4 balancing can distribute connections across equivalent ingress replicas without seeing HTTP. L7 selection needs the method, host or path after suitable HTTP/TLS processing. Use redundant ingress capacity and verify that removing one ingress still leaves enough capacity. Connection health alone cannot show that either application pool can handle requests.

## Cache behavior and origin capacity

With the brief's direct-origin path, each uncoalesced viewer-edge miss becomes one origin fetch. Warm origin demand without coalescing is 10,000 × 0.02 = **200 fetches/s**. In the cold interval it is 8,000 × 0.02 + 2,000 = **2,160 fetches/s**. If all cold requests for each key share one in-flight fetch and no second fetch starts for that key in the modeled second, there are 80 cold fetches plus 160 warm misses, or **240 fetches/s**. With four independent domains each fetching all 80 keys once, the total becomes **480 fetches/s**. A shared upstream cache can absorb some of those fetches or collapse them across domains. Requests that miss outside the overlap window and extra retries can raise the count. Put bounded rate and concurrency limits ahead of the origin and charge retries against those limits. Prefill likely keys or ramp traffic to reduce the number of cold requests delayed or rejected by admission control. Protecting origin capacity alone does **not** satisfy the viewer SLO: at least 9,900 of 10,000 original viewer requests need an eligible response within 2 seconds of their own first submission. A shed, late, failed or ineligible-stale response uses the 1% error budget even if it creates no origin fetch. A viable design needs coalescing/prefetch and measured end-to-end latency; modeled fetch counts alone do not prove the latency result.

### Conditional capacity and viewer outcomes

Consider an illustrative replay of exactly 10,000 original submissions in [0, 1 s), then no new viewer submissions. Suppose four independent domains make at most 320 cold leader attempts and the warm misses make 160 attempts across the cohort. Successful fills remain reusable for later same-key requests, and at most 40 additional retry or validation attempts reach the origin during the drain; no prefill or background attempts occur. The total is at most **520 attempts** across replay and drain, so every rolling 1-second window has at most 520, below 1,000. This is a conservative bound, not a measurement. Every attempt entering the media origin counts, including a failed attempt; attempts failing before that boundary are recorded separately. Any modeled prefill or background attempt shares the same limit.

For a passing viewer model, additionally assume each hit and each miss, including queueing, origin fetch, any retry, fan-out and transfer of the complete correct eligible variant to the client, finishes within 1.5 s of its own submission. The edge has enough fan-out capacity and reuse/overlap works as assumed. Then all 10,000 meet their individual 2 s deadlines: even a request arriving at +0.999 s completes by +2.499 s, before its +2.999 s deadline. The test must verify these timing, reuse, headroom and fan-out assumptions; the 520 bound alone says nothing about response latency. At least 9,900 timely eligible results are required; 100 failures consume the entire budget, and 101 fail the target.

For a failing contrast, suppose there is no collapsing, all 7,840 warm hits complete in time, and each of the 2,160 misses requires an attempt. An immediate-shed policy admits only 1,000 misses and sheds the other 1,160, with no eligible fallback. Even if every admitted miss completes in time, only **8,840/10,000** succeed. The attempt cap passes but the viewer target fails. Diagnose this and mitigate with verified coalescing, reusable fills or prefill charged to the same capacity budget, then measure again; if timing or reuse is unknown, the outcome is unproven rather than a pass. These are scenario assumptions, not benchmark results.

For public images, include the version and variant in the path so bytes for different sizes or formats have distinct keys. Store and serve with an explicit freshness policy. Publish a new path for replacement bytes, and give the referring catalogue metadata its own freshness and update policy. Invalidation can help when an old URL must stop being served at a controlled edge. Test how clients and intermediary caches behave when old links persist. [RFC 9111 §§4.1–4.2](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc9111#section-4.1), [CloudFront, updating existing objects](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonCloudFront/latest/DeveloperGuide/UpdatingExistingObjects.html)

With an explicit and supported `stale-if-error` policy allowing 300 seconds beyond freshness expiry, a cached public variant 20 s past freshness at the start of the 90 s outage can remain eligible throughout, reaching 110 s past freshness. A miss has no saved response to serve. Use a bounded failure or an authorized alternative origin. The saved-items view sends `Cache-Control: private, no-store` and uses a route that bypasses shared storage and stale fallback. Configure that path and origin headers together and verify with two distinct user sessions. [RFC 5861 §4](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc5861#section-4), [RFC 9111 §5.2.2](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc9111#section-5.2.2)

In a failure drill, replay the 10,000 requests/s peak against warm and simultaneously cold paths. Record edge misses, upstream cache outcomes and all fetch attempts at the media origin. Check every rolling 1-second window through the complete drain against the 1,000-attempt origin limit, counting attempts at entry across domains, including retries, validation and any modeled prefill or background work; record pre-boundary failures separately. A limiter with a nominal rate and a burst allowance needs separate evidence that it meets this hard cap. Follow every original viewer request through its **individual** two-second deadline, including requests generated near the end of the modeled second. Report the original-request success ratio, deferred/rejected/late/time-out counts, and successful-response latency distribution separately. Record the IP used by new connections and the version served. For a stale response, compare its reported `Age` with the applicable freshness lifetime and confirm the edge's stale-on-error indicator.

## Self-evaluation

Award 2 for complete reasoning, 1 for a correct but incomplete argument and 0 for a missing or contradictory argument.

| Criterion | Evidence for full credit |
| --- | --- |
| DNS trace | Correct R1/R2 outcomes and time math, with a concrete source of divergence from the DNS timeline. |
| Request path | Separates DNS, transport, TLS and HTTP. Traces hit/miss and private traffic and justifies the L4/L7 placement. |
| Origin capacity | Computes 200, 2,160, 240 and 480 attempts/s under the stated assumptions; explains upstream hits, overlap and retries; and defends a policy that bounds all origin-entry attempts in every rolling second through drain. |
| Viewer success | Independently shows at least 9,900 original requests receive complete eligible responses by their own deadlines under explicit, credible timing and reuse assumptions. Counts retries once per original and includes shedding, failure and late responses. |
| Freshness and privacy | Explains variant keys, replacement and distinct policies for eligible stale, cold and private traffic. |
| Verification | Specifies a peak cold-start test, counts fetches and retries at the origin against the limit and checks the original-viewer success denominator, deadline and shed/late traffic separately. Tests destination and freshness during failure and verifies private response isolation. |

For the capacity design to pass, both origin capacity and viewer success must earn 2 points independently. Award 1 for a sound diagnosis of failure or insufficient evidence with a relevant mitigation, even when feasibility is not established; an unsupported success claim or cap-only argument earns 0 for viewer success. A justified alternative that meets both checks earns full credit.

Other protocols, routing and cache designs can earn full credit when their paths and assumptions meet the requirements. A design that exposes a private response through a shared cache fails the access requirement.
