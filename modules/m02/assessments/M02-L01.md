# DNS and endpoint discovery assessment

## Practical exercise

EX-1. A reporting service routes to East and West. Its authoritative DNS service selects eligible answers with weights 2 and 1. One recursive resolver serves 800 clients and another serves 100, each issuing one request per interval. The authoritative service selects once for each resolver, and each resolver caches the returned answer. East fails at t = 0. The larger resolver received East at t = -30 s with a 300-second TTL. Assume ordinary TTL honoring, no stale serving and new connections for this trace. The service objective is for at least 99% of requests from each affected client group to succeed within 60 seconds of the failure.

An independently operated stable ingress address can, if deployed and reachable by clients, redirect traffic to a ready West region within an assumed 20 seconds. West and the ingress each have sufficient capacity in this scenario. Compare DNS-only failover with a design using that ingress. Trace the larger resolver through t = 60 s and its cache expiry. Explain the weighted answer-selection shares and why the request split differs. State additional assumptions and failure consequences, then name a drill and the measurements needed to substantiate the objective. Consider how existing connections or serve-stale could change the result outside the simplified trace.

## Knowledge checks

### Q1

What does a recursive resolver need to obtain when it encounters a delegation it has not cached?

**Answer:** It follows the delegation to locate the child zone's authoritative name servers and queries toward an answer.

**Explanation:** The parent delegation provides NS information for the next authority.

### Q2

A CNAME for api.example points to edge.example. What additional information is needed before an IPv4 client can connect?

**Answer:** The client or resolver needs an IPv4 address for the canonical target.

**Explanation:** The alias gives a canonical name, while an A record supplies an IPv4 address.

### Q3

At time zero an operator changes an authoritative answer. A resolver cached the old record 40 seconds earlier with TTL 120 seconds. Assuming ordinary TTL honoring, no stale service and no reused connection, can a new request at t = 50 s still use the old answer?

**Answer:** Yes. The cached record expires at t = 80 s.

**Explanation:** Its age at the request is 90 seconds, less than the 120-second TTL.

### Q4

Why does reducing the configured TTL immediately before maintenance fail to speed up every cached answer?

**Answer:** Answers already cached under the old TTL retain their remaining cache lifetime.

**Explanation:** The new TTL applies when a resolver obtains the newer record.

### Q5

A service needs an approximate 3-to-1 rollout across two eligible regions. Which DNS option fits, and what outcome must the team avoid assuming?

**Answer:** A 3-to-1 weighted answer policy fits the coarse rollout. It does not assure a 3-to-1 share of individual requests.

**Explanation:** Each resolver may reuse one selected answer for a different number of clients and requests.

### Q6

How does a geolocation policy differ from a latency policy, and what must a team verify?

**Answer:** Geolocation selects by inferred geographic location, while latency selection uses the provider's latency data. Verify the resulting client experience and policy fallback.

**Explanation:** The inferred location and provider measurements may not match a particular client's end-to-end latency.

## Answer key / rubric

The authoritative service targets two thirds East and one third West of eligible answer selections. Resolver sharing and the small number of selections mean those shares do not determine individual request shares. The larger resolver's East answer expires at t = 270 s. At t = 60 s it can still return East despite a successful authoritative switch to West, so DNS-only failover cannot meet the objective for that group under the stated failure.

One design gives clients an ingress address before the failure and routes through the separately operated ingress to the ready West region. Under the scenario's assumed 20-second reroute, sufficient capacity, successful client connections and no other failure, recovery can meet the 60-second objective. It adds an ingress dependency and cost. Test client reachability to the ingress and ensure its health decision, West readiness and capacity hold under the failure. If a client still uses an East-only address, the ingress design needs an independent way to reach it. A planned drain while East remains available is another useful operational choice but does not solve an unexpected East outage under these assumptions. Long-lived connections or serve-stale can extend migration and need separate handling and measurement.

In a failure drill, measure the time from the injected East loss to successful responses, the per-client-group success rate, p99 latency, West and ingress saturation, and traffic still reaching East. Exercise cached answers, reused connections and resolver refresh failures. Treat results as evidence for the observed conditions, not a universal DNS guarantee.

Score the exercise out of 10 points.

- 2 points for authoritative selection proportions and resolver-sharing effects.
- 2 points for the t = 270 s expiry and the t = 60 s SLO consequence.
- 3 points for a feasible alternative, its reachability and capacity assumptions, and an ingress or other failure trade-off.
- 3 points for a failure drill with per-group success and timing measurements plus a relevant connection or stale-answer condition.

A plausible routing choice earns the applicable design credit. Full credit also requires the cache and failure trace, explicit assumptions, and evidence that could establish the scenario's recovery objective. Accept other designs that meet those constraints.
