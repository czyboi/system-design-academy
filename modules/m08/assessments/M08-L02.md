# Companion assessment for M08-L02

## Practical exercise

**EX-1.** A dashboard counts distinct product requests by event ID for the event-time hour `[12:00, 13:00)`. IDs are globally unique and retries use the same ID and payload. Publish after each accepted event, publish at 13:00, accept new IDs processed strictly before 13:05 and publish a final live version at 13:05. Events processed at or after 13:05 enter a correction stream. All events and corrections are independently archived for replay. Break ties by product ID ascending.

| Processing time | Event `(ID, event time, product)` |
| --- | --- |
| 12:10 | `(a, 12:09, ink)` |
| 12:20 | `(b, 12:19, paper)` |
| 12:50 | `(c, 12:49, ink)` |
| 13:01 | retry of `a` |
| 13:04 | `(d, 12:58, paper)` |
| 13:05 | `(e, 12:45, paper)` |

1. Give the counts and leader at 13:00, after d, and at finalization. Explain what happens to e, and calculate a corrected result if the archive contains all five unique IDs.
2. State a replay and sink contract that avoids double counting and stops a delayed older repair overwriting the newer result. State the retention cost of allowing more live lateness.
3. In a different complete window, feed the two-counter Space-Saving summary the unit events `A, B, A, C`. Show its final counter estimates and error bounds. Explain whether it proves the top item and whether exact counts are required for a close decision.
4. Current counts and comparable hourly baselines are ink 60 versus 30 and paper 20 versus 2. Rank both by count and by growth ratio. Explain how a minimum-volume rule changes the decision.
5. Choose Storm Core 2.8.1 or Spark Structured Streaming 4.0.1 for this service and describe the required source, processing and sink recovery conditions. Another defensible choice earns full credit if those conditions are addressed.

## Knowledge checks

### Q1

Why does the lesson's event e4 change the 10:00 hour after 11:00?

**Answer.** Its event time is 10:54, so it belongs to `[10:00, 11:00)`. The example accepts new IDs processed before 11:05 and publishes a revised provisional result.

### Q2

The lesson's retry of e1 arrives with the same ID and payload at 11:02. What happens?

**Answer.** The retained seen-ID state identifies a duplicate, so the count stays unchanged. This is a business-ID rule even if the retry has a different source offset.

### Q3

What does a watermark establish about an event timestamp earlier than the watermark?

**Answer.** It is an estimate of event-time progress, not proof that no earlier event can arrive. The processing system's window and cleanup policy determines the effect of a later arrival.

### Q4

Why can a two-counter Space-Saving result leave a tie uncertain?

**Answer.** A replacement inherits the old minimum in its estimate and records that amount as possible error. Overlapping frequency bounds can leave order unresolved. Exact counts can settle a decision that requires a definitive ranking.

### Q5

Why might a topic with the most lifetime requests fail to be the most trending topic this hour?

**Answer.** Lifetime total measures accumulated popularity. A trend policy compares the current window with its chosen baseline, so a smaller topic with a larger relative increase can rank higher.

### Q6

Does a Storm acknowledgement or Spark checkpoint alone prevent duplicate external effects?

**Answer.** Neither alone establishes the whole external effect contract. Storm needs a reliable re-emitting spout and appropriately anchored acknowledgements, and a retry-safe sink. Spark's replay guarantee depends on compatible sources and sinks. A separate event-ID rule handles duplicate business events.

## Reference solution and rubric

At 13:00 ink has 2 and paper has 1, so ink leads. The retry of a is ignored. At 13:04 paper becomes 2, producing a tie with ink 2. Alphabetical tie-breaking keeps ink first. The final live result at 13:05 is the same. Event e is processed at the cutoff, so it goes to the correction stream. A complete archived replay deduplicated by ID gives ink 2 and paper 3, so paper leads in a corrected revision.

A repair reads the complete retained source and corrections, resolves ID conflicts and produces a replacement result. A durable coordinator assigns a higher revision for the hour, and the sink accepts a replacement only when its revision exceeds the stored one. A larger live acceptance horizon retains both per-key counts and seen IDs longer. Checkpoints and compatible sinks protect replayed processing, while business-ID deduplication handles repeated events under different offsets.

The summary ends with `(A, 2, 0)` and `(C, 2, 1)`. A's true count is 2, and C's is between 1 and 2. The estimates alone do not establish whether C ties A. Exact replay gives A 2, B 1 and C 1. Ink leads by count, 60 versus 20. Paper leads by growth, 10 times baseline versus ink's 2 times. A minimum-current-volume threshold of 10 admits both, while one above 20 excludes paper. A zero baseline needs an explicit separate policy.

For Spark, one defensible design uses a replayable source, checkpointed micro-batches and a compatible sink that supports the required replacement semantics. Deduplicate stable business IDs for the defined live horizon and use the archived repair path beyond it. For Storm Core, use a reliable spout capable of re-emitting, correctly anchor and acknowledge tuple trees, and make the sink retry-safe. Both choices require the stated correction and revision contract.
