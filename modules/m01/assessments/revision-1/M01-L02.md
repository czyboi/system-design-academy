# Performance versus scalability — companion assessment

Q4's queueing reasoning is taught in **Queueing and saturation**. Q5's state-placement reasoning is taught in **Core concepts and mechanisms**. The outcome map identifies **Trade-offs and failure modes** for the combined decision.

## Practical exercise

**EX-1.** Compare a single 120 requests/s server with two 80 requests/s servers. Work arrives at 90/s normally and 150/s for 12 seconds. Assume equal work per request, initially empty queues, stateless workers and a perfect 50/50 split in the two-server case. After the burst, arrivals return to 90/s. Each successful request also needs one operation on a shared backend.

Calculate the single-server backlog and its drain time. Determine the offered rate to each of the two servers during the burst. Then consider a separate scenario where the balanced front end dispatches all 150 backend operations/s during the burst, even while prior operations are waiting, and the backend completes 110 operations/s. Choose a scaling action, name one failure condition that could change it, and list two measurements needed to test the choice.

## Knowledge checks

### Q1

What do latency and throughput measure?

Answer: Latency measures elapsed time for an operation. Throughput measures completed work per unit of time.

Explanation: Both need a stated workload and population of operations to support a comparison.

### Q2

At a constant 85 arrivals/s and 70 completions/s, how much backlog accumulates in eight seconds?

Answer: 120 requests.

Explanation: The queue grows at 15/s for eight seconds under the simplified model.

### Q3

After the burst, arrivals are 50/s and capacity is 70/s. How long does the 120-request backlog take to drain?

Answer: 6 seconds.

Explanation: The queue drains at a net 20 requests/s, so 120 ÷ 20 is 6 seconds.

### Q4

Why can successful throughput increase while tail latency worsens as offered load rises?

Answer: More requests may complete per second while waiting queues grow.

Explanation: Each queued request accumulates waiting time before service. A few especially slow requests can increase the tail.

### Q5

What could limit a horizontal scale-out when session data lives only in one worker's memory?

Answer: A different worker might not have the required session state.

Explanation: Affinity or external state can support routing, with trade-offs in skew, failure recovery and dependencies.

### Q6

A successful-request p99 falls after a deployment, but timeouts double. What should the team inspect before concluding the user experience improved?

Answer: Inspect offered load, successful completions, error and timeout rates, and latency for a comparable request population.

Explanation: Requests that time out can disappear from the successful-request latency sample while fewer users receive a successful response.

## Answer key / rubric

The single server accumulates (150 − 120) × 12 = 360 requests and drains them at 120 − 90 = 30/s, or 12 seconds. Each of two balanced servers receives 75/s during the burst, below its assumed 80/s capacity. In the separate shared-backend scenario, the explicitly assumed 150 operations/s dispatch rate exceeds 110/s backend capacity by 40/s, so the backend accumulates 480 operations in twelve seconds. With a single 120/s front end, some offered work could queue before reaching the backend.

An acceptable decision is to investigate and increase the backend's useful capacity before adding front-end workers. Measure backend queue depth and successful end-to-end p99 under the same workload, along with errors and offered load. Check the capacity remaining after a front-end instance fails, since a single 80/s server cannot sustain the stated normal offered load. A different decision is acceptable when its assumptions and target are explicit.

Award 10 points: 3 for single-server backlog and drain, 2 for the balanced split, 2 for the backend bottleneck, and 3 for a justified change, failure condition and two measurements. Accept equivalent arithmetic and defensible alternatives.
