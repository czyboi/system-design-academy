# Performance versus scalability — companion assessment

## Practical exercise

**EX-1.** Compare a single 100 requests/s server with two 65 requests/s servers. Work arrives at 70/s normally and 120/s for 10 seconds. Assume equal work per request, initially empty queues, stateless workers and a perfect 50/50 split in the two-server case. After the burst, arrivals return to 70/s. Each successful request also needs one operation on a shared backend.

Calculate the single-server backlog and its drain time. Determine the offered rate to each of the two servers during the burst. Then consider a separate scenario where the shared backend completes at most 90 operations/s. Choose a scaling action, name one failure condition that could change it, and list two measurements needed to test the choice.

## Knowledge checks

### Q1

What do latency and throughput measure?

Answer: Latency measures elapsed time for an operation. Throughput measures completed work per unit of time.

Explanation: Both need a stated workload and population of operations to support a comparison.

### Q2

At a constant 120 arrivals/s and 100 completions/s, how much backlog accumulates in ten seconds?

Answer: 200 requests.

Explanation: The queue grows at 20/s for ten seconds under the simplified model.

### Q3

After the burst, arrivals are 70/s and capacity is 100/s. How long does the 200-request backlog take to drain?

Answer: About 6.7 seconds.

Explanation: The queue drains at a net 30 requests/s, so 200 ÷ 30 is approximately 6.7 seconds.

### Q4

Why can successful throughput increase while tail latency worsens as offered load rises?

Answer: More requests may complete per second while waiting queues grow.

Explanation: Each queued request accumulates waiting time before service. A few especially slow requests can increase the tail.

### Q5

What could limit a horizontal scale-out when session data lives only in one worker's memory?

Answer: A different worker might not have the required session state.

Explanation: Affinity or external state can support routing, with trade-offs in skew, failure recovery and dependencies.

### Q6

A successful-request p99 falls after a deployment, but timeouts double. What should the team inspect before concluding the user experience improved?

Answer: Inspect offered load, successful completions, error and timeout rates, and latency for a comparable request population.

Explanation: Requests that time out can disappear from the successful-request latency sample while fewer users receive a successful response.

## Answer key / rubric

The single server accumulates (120 − 100) × 10 = 200 requests and drains them at 100 − 70 = 30/s, or about 6.7 seconds. Each of two balanced servers receives 60/s during the burst, below its assumed 65/s capacity. In the separate shared-backend scenario, a 120/s burst exceeds 90/s backend capacity by 30/s, so the backend can accumulate up to 300 requests over ten seconds in the simplified model.

An acceptable decision is to investigate and increase the backend's useful capacity before adding front-end workers. Measure backend queue depth and successful end-to-end p99 under the same workload, along with errors and offered load. Check the capacity remaining after a front-end instance fails, since a single 65/s server cannot sustain the stated normal offered load. A different decision is acceptable when its assumptions and target are explicit.

Award 10 points: 3 for single-server backlog and drain, 2 for the balanced split, 2 for the backend bottleneck, and 3 for a justified change, failure condition and two measurements. Accept equivalent arithmetic and defensible alternatives.
