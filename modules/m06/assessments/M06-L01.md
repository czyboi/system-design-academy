# Companion assessment for M06-L01

## Practical exercise

**EX-1.** A venue catalogue receives 1,000 searches/s at the application. Public English and Spanish results can be shared within their variants. Staff results include restricted listings. Assume the effective warm hit ratio is 85%: searches served by the application result cache divided by all application searches, including staff bypasses in the denominator. Each search not served by that cache, including a bypass, produces one origin query in the warm baseline. The origin can execute at most 200 catalogue queries/s. A 48 MiB cache reserves 25% for headroom and the hot set has 9,000 entries at 4 KiB each, including overhead. One newly listed venue joins several existing search results.

Specify cache keys and location, budget the working set and warm origin load, and trace a first search, a match introduced by the new venue, and popular-key expiry during a scale-out. For staff results, justify the access boundary. Pick an update policy and explain why another is a poor fit.

## Knowledge checks

### Q1

What expensive work can a result-cache hit avoid even if all the required database pages are resident?

**Answer.** The application can reuse the finished result and avoid running filtering, sorting or aggregation again. Resident pages help the database execute that work with less I/O.

### Q2

At 1,000 lookups/s with a 90% hit rate, how many origin queries/s occur if each miss goes to the origin exactly once?

**Answer.** 100 queries/s. Coalescing, intermediate result caches and retries would change that conversion.

### Q3

Why can adding readers during a cold start increase origin load even when the query mix stays fixed?

**Answer.** Local copies on fresh replicas start empty and fills can multiply across replicas. One shared coalescer limits simultaneous fills of a given key in its scope.

### Q4

A photo credit changes on an exhibit present in many catalogue results. How does caching cards by ID change invalidation?

**Answer.** Evict or version the affected card rather than every copy of that card's payload. A separate cached search result still needs invalidation if the change affects membership or order.

### Q5

Why might the old result payload be insufficient for Query X invalidation after a new matching record is added?

**Answer.** The new ID is absent from the old payload. Use a dependency representation that covers possible membership changes or bump the affected query namespace.

### Q6

Why might refresh-ahead be a poor fit for personalized one-off searches?

**Answer.** It can consume origin capacity refreshing keys that nobody will read again. A lazy fill or no result caching fits low reuse better.

## Sample solution and rubric

The working set is 36,000 KiB, about 35.16 MiB. It fits in the 36 MiB allocation with about 0.84 MiB of margin under the assumed sizes. Warm origin load is 150 queries/s. The baseline needs at least an 80% application hit rate to stay at 200 queries/s, leaving no spare capacity at that boundary. If the shared cache is entirely cold, offered demand before protection is 1,000 queries/s under the one-query-per-search assumption. This is not necessarily the admitted rate.

Authorize before key selection. Use a shared application cache for public results keyed by normalized query, locale, page, visibility scope and generation. Bypass shared reuse for staff results unless their authorized audience can be encoded and checked on every lookup. A first miss reads the shared generation, queries the PostgreSQL 18 primary using a fresh Read Committed statement, and stores the result under that captured generation. Commit the new venue and an outbox event together; a retried, duplicate-safe consumer advances the affected search domain, including pages that previously omitted the venue. Before advancement, old results can be served; a late old-generation fill stays under its old key. After advancement, the new primary statement sees the commit. Authoritative authorization checks protect revocation throughout.

A new replica can immediately hit a warm shared entry. When a shared popular key expires, one fleet-wide fill owner queries and waiters use its result within their deadlines. Assume the entire 200 queries/s capacity is available to this workload: pace all fleet-wide origin starts, including staff bypasses, at no more than one every 5 ms (200/s, no burst), and separately cap in-flight work. Excess requests or waiters whose deadlines expire receive a retryable error; they do not bypass the limit. A concurrency cap alone cannot enforce the rate. Cache-aside suits this read-heavy result. Eagerly rewriting every affected result would amplify a single catalogue write.
